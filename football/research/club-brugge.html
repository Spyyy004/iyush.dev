Football research · Memo

How much of a player's game travels?

A public-data test of cross-league recruitment

Prepared for
Jan Van HaarenDirector of Football Intelligence, Club Brugge
By
Ayush PawarOctober 2026 · interactive version: iyush.dev/football/research/club-brugge

Similarity finds candidates.
Player history predicts performance.

Independent research from public event data, written for Club Brugge because I think the question is relevant to its work. It was not commissioned by the club and uses none of its data.

01

The recruitment problem

A forward produces 0.65 xG + xA per 90 in league A. What should a recruitment department expect from him in league B?

A player's numbers don't belong entirely to the player. League difficulty, the strength of the club around him, his age and the shape of his recent seasons all leave a mark. There are three obvious ways to make the forecast:

  1. Trust his previous output.
  2. Adjust for the destination: the league and the club.
  3. Find similar players and look at what they did.

I tested all three on transfers the model had never seen.

Why Club Brugge

Club Brugge describes its mission as becoming a 100% evidence-based organisation, and its football data science team is free to build “new metrics, models, and long-term projects”, including “models of how football and the transfer market are evolving”.

The recruitment challenge, in Jan Van Haaren's words, is to get players “on your radar as soon as possible”. Data shrinks the pool to something scouts can watch. That makes the question here not only who is good, but what a player's numbers will become in a new environment.

Quotes: Jan Van Haaren, interview with Total Football Analysis, 18 February 2026.

02

The data

  • 10seasons, 2015/16–2024/25
  • 5leagues: EPL, La Liga, Serie A, Bundesliga, Ligue 1
  • 18,011matches
  • 8,348players
  • 525,328player-matches
  • 2,917cross-league moves, inferred from club spells
  • 428in the primary transfer sample

The primary sample is every summer move between two of the five leagues by a forward or midfielder with at least 900 minutes before and after it, with destination seasons from 2016/17 to 2025/26. Retention is output after the move against what comparable players who stayed at their club produced the next season: same role, same pre-move output, same age. That strips out regression to the mean and normal ageing.

This is public, match-level event data: shots, xG and xA from Understat, plus club and league context. It has no defensive actions, possession, passes, carries, tracking, physical data, injuries, contracts or fees. Every forecast below is about attacking output only.

03

A move doesn't automatically destroy a player's output

Across all 428 moves, players kept 97% of their xG + xA per 90 against comparable stayers (95% CI 93%–101%). There is no universal “league-change penalty”. The direction is what matters:

One ladder of league difficulty explains all 20 directions: Premier League hardest, Serie A and La Liga in the middle, the Bundesliga and Ligue 1 easiest. Pair-specific effects add nothing (F-test p = 0.91). Moving into the Premier League keeps 82%; the same player moving the other way would be expected to gain.

04

The league isn't everything

Within that ladder, three things about the player and the destination club matter about as much as the league itself.

Destination club

79% → 106%

Weakest third of destination clubs vs strongest third. A step up in club strength keeps 114%; a step down, 81%.

Age

−2%

Retention per extra year of age, beyond normal ageing (95% CI −3.2% to −0.8%).

A recent jump

−9%

Per standard deviation of last-season improvement. Players arriving off a spike come down further.

The destination isn't a league. It's a league, a club and a player's trajectory.

05

The obvious next idea: find players like him

If output changes in a new environment, perhaps we should look at players who play like the one we're buying. That was Study 4: a cross-league similarity engine on nine context-adjusted metrics, z-scored within role, compared by cosine similarity.

It finds sensible candidates. Searching Serie A for Bruno Fernandes returns Samardžić, Dybala and Chukwueze as the matches that survive every specification. It also shows the limit of similarity: Bruno's chance creation sits 3.6 standard deviations above his role average; the best in Serie A's pool is 1.9. The matches share his shape, not his level.

Similarity can find candidates. Does it help predict them?

06

The experiment

Two tests, both leak-free: every feature is frozen at the summer of the move and each model only sees earlier moves.

Test 1 · How much does context and history buy?

Four models, specified on 286 earlier moves and then scored once on 142 transfers from 2023/24–2025/26 that had been locked away.

Context and the player's own three-season history cut the error by a quarter against “he'll do what he did before” (0.129 → 0.096; difference −0.033, 95% CI −0.049 to −0.018). It also ranks well: AUC 0.80 for keeping at least 75% of output.

Test 2 · Does similarity add anything?

The same history model, plus Study 4's similarity features: what the most similar players produce in the destination league, how good the match is, how distinctive the player is, and how similar past movers fared.

Adding the comparables changes the error by 0.000.

The comparables' output is almost a restatement of the player's own level (r = 0.89), and carries nothing the history model doesn't already know. Using comparables instead of the player's history is worse, and no better than assuming nothing changes.

07

Conclusion

Similarity finds candidates.

Player history predicts performance.

I built the similarity engine expecting it to improve transfer forecasts, then tested whether it did. It didn't. Its job is discovery: shortlisting, and covering roles whose value the data can't see. The forecast should come from the player's own history in context.

08

What this model cannot answer for Club Brugge

Belgium is not in the data.
The ladder covers the top five leagues only. Club Brugge buys from leagues outside it and sells into it, so the club's own rung, and the rungs of most of its scouting markets, are exactly what this model cannot place.
The model starts at 900 minutes.
Club Brugge wants to decide whether a player is worth looking into “even when players haven't had many minutes”. The forecast needs a conventional sample before it says anything, so it does not solve that problem.
Retention is conditional on playing.
27% of league movers don't reach 900 minutes in the new league (stayers: 20%). The model says what a player produces if he plays, not whether he will.
It sees attacking output only.
No defensive actions, possession, passes, carries, pressing or physical data. Goalkeepers and defenders are out of scope.
Individual forecasts are wide.
A typical 80% interval is about ±0.2 xG + xA per 90. It covers 87% of outcomes on the locked test, so it's honest but conservative. This is a filter, not a decision.

09

If I had club data

The structure of the test (a forecast fixed before the outcome, scored on locked seasons, compared against naive baselines) carries over. What would change is what goes into it.

  1. Event dataReplace xG + xA with an action-value target, so build-up, progression and defending count, not just the last two actions of a chance.
  2. Tactical contextPossession share, team style, role and zones occupied, pressing context, how much chance creation the team gives the player.
  3. TrackingDoes off-ball behaviour travel: runs, spacing, pressing intensity? It may travel better than output does.
  4. PhysicalSprints, high-speed running, accelerations, availability, against the demands of the destination league.
  5. Video“Data tells us which video to look at, video provides context for the numbers.” The model should point the scout at the clips where it is least sure, and the scout's verdict should be logged so the model can be evaluated against it.

10

Where it fits in a recruitment workflow

I build software products for a living, so I think about this as a sequence of decisions, each with a question the data can or cannot answer.

  1. ProfileHow good is he once context is adjusted for?
  2. SimilarityWho plays like him? Discovery, not forecasting.
  3. TransferabilityWhat should we expect at our club, in our league?
  4. UncertaintyHow wide is the range? How much history is it built on?
  5. Blind spotsWhat can't this data see for this role?
  6. VideoWhich clips would settle the uncertainty?
  7. DecisionDoes he deserve a deeper look?

11

What I got wrong

Similarity
I expected comparable players to improve transfer forecasts. They didn't, and comparables alone were worse than the player's own history.
Ugarte
For Manchester United in 2024 the model predicted 0.02 xG + xA per 90 (80% range 0.01–0.05); he produced 0.14. Two causes, both named before the outcome: a fixed Premier League penalty over-punishes low-output midfielders (about 0.06 too low on the locked test), and ball-winning, which is why he was bought, is invisible to this data.
Low-minute players
The model needs 900 minutes. It doesn't address one of the most interesting recruitment problems: judging a player before a conventional sample exists.

12

The next question

Can we estimate how a player's output will transfer before he has a conventional sample of minutes, and from leagues outside the top five?

  1. Extend the difficulty ladder to the leagues Club Brugge actually scouts and sells into, Belgium included, using players who moved between them and the top five.
  2. Replace the 900-minute gate with partial pooling: shrink low-minute players toward their role and league, and report the wider interval honestly instead of hiding the player.
  3. Test it the same way as here: fit on earlier windows, lock the later ones, and publish the misses.
